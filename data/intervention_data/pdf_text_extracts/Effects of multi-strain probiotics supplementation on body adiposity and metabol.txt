Background  & aims: Recent evidence suggests that gut microbiota has a potential role in the patho- 
physiology of obesity and  other cardiovascular disease (CVD)  risk factors, including hypertension, dysli- 
pidemia, and  type 2  diabetes. How ever, clinical trials evaluating the effects of probiotics supplementation  
on  these outcomes  have found  inconsistent results, probably due  to the wide  heterogeneity in trial de- 
signs. In addition, there is a lack of studies investigating whether  probiotics can enhance  the beneficial 
effects of caloric restriction in individuals with increased risk of CVD  as individuals with hypertension and  
excess body  adiposity. Thus, the aim  of this study w as to evaluate the effects of multi-strain probiotics 
supplementation  on  body  adiposity, glycemic  homeostasis, lipid profile, and  serum  adipokine levels in 
individuals with hypertension and  excess body  weight  following an  energy restricted diet. 
Methods: A  randomized,  double-blind, placebo controlled clinical trial w as conducted  for 12  weeks. 
W ere included 66 individuals aged  between  40 and  65  years; both  sexes; body  mass  index (BMI) ≥ 25  
and  <  40 kg/m  2  and  diagnosis of hypertension. W ere excluded smokers;  individuals using probiotics, 
prebiotics, symbiotics and  antibiotics in the last 3  months;  presenting diabetes, chronic kidney  disease 
or liver failure; and  pregnant and  lactating women.  Participants wer e allocated into 2  groups:  group  
with  supplementation  of 8 probiotic strains in capsules (3  × 10 10 CFU/day)  or control group  (placebo 
capsules). Both  groups  followed  a low-calorie diet. Participants underwent  anthropometric, body  
composition  (dual-energy radiological absorptiometry)  and  biochemical (glucose metabolism,  lipid 
profile, adiponectin, and  leptin) evaluation at baseline and  at the end  of the study. 
Results: After 12  weeks  of intervention, the probiotics group  presented:  a) reduction of body  weight, 
BMI, circumferences of w aist, hip and  neck  and  w aist-to-height ratio; b)  decrease in total fat mass  (kg); 
and  c) reduction  of glycated hemoglobin  (HbA1c).  In the control group, it w as observed:  a) significant 
reduction in all anthropometric  variables; b)  significant reduction in total fat mass  (kg and  %), trunk fat 
mass  (kg), visceral fat and  load capacity index. In the comparison  between  groups, there w as a higher 
decrease in HbA1c  in the probiotics group  (p <  0.05). 
Conclusion: Multi-strain probiotics supplementation  associated with  energy  restriction in individuals 
with  excess body  w eight and  hypertension promo ted a significant improvement  in glucose homeostasis  
assessed by  HbA1c.  The  clinical trial w as registered at www .ensaiosclinicos.gov.br: RBR-7jw4ry.

1. Introduction
Over  the last decades, the prevalence of obesity has reached  
epidemic  proportions. In 2022,  43  %  of adults wer e overweight and  
16 %  lived  with  obesity wor ldwide  [1]. Obesity is associated with  
worsening  physiological and  metabolic paramet ers that contribute 
to the development  and  progression of cardiovascular disease 
(CVD)  risk factors, such  as hypertension, dyslipidemia and  type 2  
diabetes mellitus, favoring CVD  and  all-cause mortality [2 –4]. In- 
dividuals with  obesity compared  with  those presenting normal  
weight  have a 50 –100 %  higher risk of death  [5 ], while weight  loss 
has beneficial effects on  the cardiometabolic risk associated with  
overw eight [2,5 ,6]. Lifestyle interventions represent the first step 
to achieve and  maintain  an  effective weight  reduction, while en- 
ergy restriction is the cornerstone of weight  loss [7]. 
Obesity results from  the comple x interplay of multiple genetic, 
metabolic, behavioral and  environmental  factors [8]. In addition, 
accumulating  evidence suggests that altered  gut microbiota has a 
potential role in the pathogenes is of obesity and  metabolic disor- 
ders. Some  underlying mechanisms  have been  postulated, 
including chronic low-grade  inflammation  induced  by  trans- 
location of pathogen-associated  molecular  patterns (PAMPs),  as 
lipopolysaccharides. Addi tionally, gut microbiota-derived  metab-  
olites may  influence energy  metabolism,  by  regulating the ab- 
sorption and  utilization of nutrients, and  modulating  appetite, as 
well the development  and  function of adipose tissue [9–11]. 
Restoring  a healthy gut microbial composition  can have po- 
tential benefits for impro ving metabolic disorders and  reducing 
body  adiposity [12]. Gut microbiota can be modulat ed  by  pro- 
biotics [13 ], defined as “live microorganisms  that, when  adminis- 
tered  in adequate amounts,  confer a health benefit on  the host’’, 
with  adequate evidence of safety [14]. In addition, to be termed  as 
probiotic, microorganisms  need  to be able to live in conditions of 
the digestive tract, such  as acidic pH  and  the presence of digestive 
enzymes  and  biliary salts [15 ]. Although  the use of multi-strain 
probiotics compared  with  single-strain probiotic may  be consid- 
ered more  helpful because  of synergy  and  additive effects [16–19], 
there isn't a consensus  regarding its effects on  body  adiposity. 
In recent years, some  randomized  controlled trials (RCTs) 
evaluated the effects of probiotics on  adiposity, metabolic bio- 
markers, and  serum  adipokines levels in overweight/obese  in- 
dividuals, yielding inconsistent results [20 –28], which  is probably 
due  to the heterogeneity in study design (e.g. duration of the study, 
probiotic vehicle, dose  of probiotics, use of single strain or multi- 
strains). It is wo rth noting that there is a lack of studies investi- 
gating whether  probiotics can enhance  the effects of caloric re- 
striction in individuals with  increased risk of CVD  such  as 
individuals with  hypertension and  excess body  adiposity. More- 
over, there is evidence that the use of anti-hypertensive drugs may  
impact  the composition  of gut microbiota [29,30], which  can 
potentially interfere with  the effect of probiotics [3 1]. Thus, the 
aim  of this study w as to evaluate the effects of multi-strain pro- 
biotics supplementation  on  body  adiposity, serum  adipokine 
levels, glycemic  homeostasis  and  lipid profile in individuals with  
hypertension and  excess body  weight  following an  energy  
restricted  diet.
2. Methods
This is a 12-week, single-center, parallel, double-blind, pla- 
cebo-controlled randomized  trial that w as conducted  from  
December  202 1 to March 2023  at the Laboratory of Clinical and  
Experimental  Pathophysiology (CLINEX), located at the State 
U niversity of Rio de  Janeiro, Brazil. The  trial w as approved  by  the 
Committee  on  Ethics and  Research of the Pedro Ernesto  U niversity
Hospital (CAAE:  02752 118.0.0000.5259)  and  w as registered  at 
www .ensaiosclinicos.gov.br (RBR-7jw4ry). All participants pro- 
vided written informed  consent.
2.1. Subjects and  study design
Eligible participants for the trial wer e individuals with  hyper- 
tension and  overweight  or obesity who  met  the criteria described 
in Table 1. Individuals, who  met  preliminary eligibility criteria and  
agreed  to participate in the study, wer e scheduled  for a screening 
visit to assess additional inclusion/exclusion criteria. At  the 
screening visit, potential participants arrived  at the laboratory 
after a 12-h fasting period for the evaluation of clinical, anthro- 
pometric  and  laboratory paramet ers. In the next week,  individuals 
who  fulfilled all eligibility criteria wer e scheduled  for the baseline 
visit of the study (week  0; W0)  and  received  the necessary in- 
structions: 12-h fasting and  alcohol abstinence in the previous 
three days. 
At  baseline (W0), using block randomization, participants wer e 
allocated in two groups  with  a 1:1 ratio to receive capsules con- 
taining either probiotics (probiotics group)  or placebo (control 
group). Both groups  wer e evaluated at baseline (W0)  and  at weeks  
6 (W6)  and  12  (W12). At  baseline, participants underwent  
assessment  of dietary intake, anthropometric, body  composition  
and  laboratory data. During  this visit, patients received  in- 
structions to follow an  energy  restricted  diet and  received  the 
capsules (with or without  probiotics) for the first six weeks  of the 
study. At  W6,  they underwent  anthropom etric and  body  compo-  
sition evaluation, and  received  the capsules for the next six weeks. 
At  W12, they repeated all assessments  carried out at W0.
2.2. Randomization and  blinding
A  comput er-generated  random  number  sequence  w as utilized 
to determine  group  allocation. The  random  allocation sequence  
w as generated by  a researcher who  neither enrolled participants in 
the study nor assigned participants to the sequence  of in- 
terventions. The  allocation sequence  also w as concealed, using 
sequentially numbered  opaque, sealed envelopes that wer e 
opened  only after irreversible assignment  to a participant. Both 
participants and  researchers rema ined blinded to group  allocation 
throughout  the study. Group  assignment  w as kept concealed until 
the statistical analyses wer e compleated.

2.3. Probiotic intervention
The  probiotics group  received  capsules containing eight freeze- 
dried probiotic strains: Lactobacillus acidophilus (La-14) 12  × 10 9 
UFC, Bifidobacterium lactis (Bl-04) 12  × 10 9 UFC, Bifidobacterium 
longum  (Bl-05) 1 × 10 9 UFC, Lactobacillus casei (Lc-11) 1 × 10 9 UFC, 
Lactobacillus rhamnosus  (Lr-32)  1 × 10 9 UFC, Lactobacillus planta- 
rum  (Lp-115)  1 × 10 9 UFC, Lactobacillus salivarius (Ls-33)  1 × 10 9 
UFC, Bifidobacterium brevis (Bb-03) 1 × 10 9 UFC, total dose  of 
3  × 10 10 UFC, and  microcrystalline cellulose excipient. The  placebo 
group  received  similar capsules (color, shape  and  size) without  
probiotic strains (microcrystalline cellulose). 
Both  groups  wer e instructed to consume  one  capsule a day, 
30  min  prior to breakfast. Compliance  with  the intervention w as 
assessed using the indirect method  of capsule counting. During  the 
study period, participants wer e advised to refrain from  products 
containing probiotics, prebiotics or symbiotics as well as food rich 
in polyphenols.
2.4. Dietary prescription
Both  groups  wer e advised to adhere to an  energy  restricted  diet 
throughout  the 12-week  study period. The  total energy  value (TEV)  
of the diet prescribed for each  participant w as calculated  by  
reducing their estimated daily energy  expenditure by  800 kcal 
with  the latter being determine d using Dietary Reference Intake 
(DRI) equations based  on  their current body  weight  [32 ]. The  
macronutrients  distribution w as set as follows: carbohydrates 
50 –60 %  of TEV,  protein 15 –20  %  of TEV  and  lipids 25 –30  %  of TEV.  
At  weeks  6 and  12, TEV  of the diet w as modified  according to the 
participant's updated  body  weight  at these wee ks, as an  attempt to 
maintain  weight  loss.
2.5. Assessment of dietary intake
The  dietary intake w as evaluated through  three interviewer- 
administered  24-h dietary recalls (covering two  weekda ys and  
one  weeken d day)  performed  at baseline (W0)  and  at the end  of 
the study (W12). At  W0  and  W12  trained dietitians conducted  the 
first two 24h recalls in person, and  the third via telephone  
communication.  The  three 24h  dietary recalls collected at W0  
wer e used  to evaluate habitual consumption,  whereas  the three 
24h  dietary recalls at W12  wer e utilized to assess adherence  to the 
recommended  diet. 
The  dietary recalls wer e conducted  by  two qualified dietitians, 
who  instructed participants to list all foods and  beverages 
consumed  from  midnight  to midnight  in the previous day, 
including the portion sizes. 
The  nutritional analysis of the 24h  dietary recalls w as con- 
ducted  using the software Food  Processor Plus® (ESHA  Research, 
Salem, Oregon), supplemented  by  data from  two  Brazilian food 
composition  Tables [3 3 ,34].
2.6. Anthropometric measures
The  anthropometric  measur ements  wer e conducted  in dupli- 
cate by  two  qualified dietitians, with  averaged  results being used  
for analysis. The  height w as determined  using a calibrated  stadi- 
ometer  (±0.5 cm  accuracy), while body  weight w as measur ed  with
a digital scale (±0.1 kg accuracy; Filizola S.A., S ~ ao Paulo, SP, Brazil).
Measurements  wer e taken with  participants wearing  lightweight 
clothing, without  footwear, and  after emptying  the bladder. Body  
mass  index (BMI) w as derived  from  the standard equation (kg/m  2  )
[35 ]. W aist circumference  (WC) measurement  w as obtained by  
participants in the standing position, using the midpoint  between  
the lowest rib and  the iliac crest as the reference  point, recorded  
during normal  exhalation. For hip circumference  (HC) assessment, 
w as measured  at the maximal  protrusion of the gluteal region with  
the tape parallel to the floor [36 ]. W aist-to-hip ratio w as then 
computed  by  dividing W C (cm)  by  HC  (cm). The  w aist-to-height 
ratio w as calculated by  dividing W C (cm)  by  height (cm). Neck 
circumference  (NC) measur ement  w as taken at the midw ay of the 
neck  between  mid-cervical spine and  mid-anterior neck, posi- 
tioned just below  the laryngeal prominence  when  identifiable. 
Participants stayed  in the standing position with  the head  aligned 
with  the Frankfurt horizontal plane during this assessment  [3 7].
2.7. Dual-energy  X-ray absorptiometry
Body  composition  analysis w as conducted  through  dual-energy 
X-ray absorptiometry  (DXA)  using a GE  Medical SystemsLunar ® 
device (Madison, Wisconsin,USA), operated  by  a certified techni- 
cian. Patients wer e in a supine position during the examination  
while the system  conducted  longitudinal rectilinear scanning 
along the body's axis. The  scanning sequence  initiates at the cra- 
nial region and  progresses caudally to the lower extrem ities The  
software computes  fat mass, lean tissue and  bone  mineral mass. 
Body  composition  w as evaluated in total body  and  different sites, 
such  as trunk. Visceral adipose tissue quantification w as per- 
formed  using the software CoreScan VA T  [38]. Additionally, the 
load-capacity index (LCI), which  integrates the effects of body  
adiposity and  lean soft tissue to predict disease risk and  w as 
derived  by  dividing total fat mass  (kg) by  total lean soft tissue (kg) 
[39 ].
2.8. Habitual physical activity
Habitual physical activity level w as assessed through  the In- 
ternational Physical Activity Questionnaire (Short Form). This 
validated tool [40] w as used  during the screening phase, with  only 
sedentary or irregularly active participants being eligible for in- 
clusion. All study participants wer e instructed not to change  their 
usual physical activity throughout  the investigation period.
2.9. Biochemical parameters
Laboratory paramet ers included glucose, insulin, glycosylated  
hemoglobin  A1c  (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), high-density li- 
poprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides (TGs), adiponectin, 
leptin, glutamic oxalacetic transaminase  (GOT)  and  glutamic py- 
ruvic transaminase  (GPT)  enzymes.  Glucose w as determined  by  
glucose hexokinase method.  Insulin w as determine d by  electro- 
chemiluminescence  immunoassa y using commercial  kit (Roche  
Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). HbA1c  w as determined  by  
turbidimetric inhibition method.  TC, HDL-C, TGs, uric acid, total 
protein and  albumin  concentrations wer e assessed by  using the 
enzymatic  colorimetric method  (Wiener Lab Group, Argen tina). 
Creatinine and  urea levels wer e determined  by  kinetic method.  
Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) wer e estimated by  
using the Friedewald formula  [41]. Adiponectin  and  leptin wer e 
assessed by  Enzyme-Linked  Immunosorbent  Assay. GOT  and  GPT  
wer e determined  by  the kinetics in ultra-violet (Wiener Lab Group , 
Argentina). Insulin resistance status w as assessed using the ho- 
meostasis model  assessment  of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR)  in- 
dex, which  wer e calculated  as fasting insulin (μ U/ml)  × fasting 
plasma  glucose (mmol/l)/22.5  [42].

2.10. Statistical methods
The  Shapiro–Wilk test w as used  to verify whether  the contin- 
uous  variables had  normal  distribution. The  continuous  variables 
with  normal  distribution wer e presented as mean  values and  
standard deviation, while the variables without  normal  distribu- 
tion wer e presented  as median  and  interquartile range. Categorical 
variables wer e expressed as absolute number  and  relative fre- 
quency. Comparisons  between  the two  groups  at baseline (W0)  
wer e performed  using the unpaired t test (variables with  normal  
distribution), Mann  Whitney  test (variables without  normal  dis- 
tribution) or chi-square test (categorical variables). 
Two-w ay repeated  measure s analysis of variance (ANO VA)  w as 
used  to evaluate the effect of each  group  (probiotics and  control) 
regardless of time  (group effect), the effect of time  independently  
of the group  (time effect), and  the interaction betwee n both factors 
(group and  time), indicative of the difference  of the response  be- 
tween  the two groups  (group  vs. time  effect or treatment  effect). In 
the present study we  considered that the intergroup  analysis w as 
performed  by  two-w ay repeated measures  ANO VA.  While  one- 
w ay repeated measures  ANO VA  w as performed  to evaluate the 
effect of time  in each  group  separately (intragroup  analysis). For 
variables showi ng  significant differences between  groups  at 
baseline (W0), we  performed  a linear regression analysis to 
determine  whether  the changes observed  during the study period 
wer e independent  of the baseline values. 
Outcome  data wer e analyzed on  an  intention to treat (ITT) 
basis, and  the effect size w as estimated as eta squared  (ƞ 2  ), and  
Cohen's definitions of small (0.01), medium  (0.06), and  large (0.14) 
effect sizes wer e applied [43]. The  statistical analysis w as per- 
formed  with  the software STA TA  version 13.0, adop ting signifi- 
cance level p-value < 0.05. 
This wor k w as part of a more  compreh ensive research project, 
which  aimed  to evaluate, in individuals with  hypertension and  
overw eight or obesity, following energy  restriction, the effects of 
probiotics supplementation  on  blood pressure. Thus, the sample  
size w as calculated  based  on  the study by  He  et al. (2017) [44], in 
which  a significant reduction  in blood pressure w as observed  after 
intervention with  probiotics. To detect a 5-mmHg  reduction in 
systolic blood pressure in the probiotics group, a minimum  of 24 
participants in each  group  w as necessary, assuming  a type 1 error 
of 0.05 and  a pow er of 80 %.
3. Results
Among  the interviewed  individuals, 66 met  the eligibility 
criteria, agreed  to participate and  thus wer e randoml y allocated in 
the control group  (n = 34)  or probiotics group  (n = 32).  During  the 
study, 15  follow-up losses wer e recorded. Thus, 51 individuals 
completed  the study, 28 in the control group  and  23  in the pro- 
biotics group  (Fig. 1). 
Demographic,  lifestyle, anthropome tric, clinical and  laboratory 
characteristics at baseline (W0), according to study group, are 
presented  in Table 2. The  two  groups  (control vs. probiotics) wer e 
comparable  in terms  of age, sex, lifestyle habits, anthropometry , 
blood pressure, use of antihypertensive drugs, and  laboratory pa- 
rameters, except for TGs  that we re significantly lower in the con- 
trol group  (106 (80–127) mg/dL  vs. 158 (105 –201) mg/dL;  
p = 0.004). 
The  values of anthropometric  variables at baseline, after 6 
weeks  and  after 12  weeks, according to the study group  are pre- 
sented in Table 3 . Considering the changes in anthropome tric 
variables throughout  the study in each  group  separately (one-way 
repeated  measures  ANO VA),  we  observed  a significant reduction of 
all anthropometric  variables in the control group. In the probiotics
group, there w as also a significant reduction in anthropometric  
variables, except for BMI, that presented a p-value close to sta- 
tistical significance (p = 0.05). When  we  analyzed the data of the 
two  groups  together, the two-way repeated  measures  ANO VA  
revealed a significant effect of time  (time effect p <  0.001) for all 
anthropometric  paramet ers, with  no  significant difference  be- 
tween  groups  and  without  significant effect of the interaction 
group  vs. time  (effect of probiotics supplementation), which  sug- 
gests that weigh t loss w as due  to energy  restriction rather than 
probiotics supplementation. 
In Table 4, the values of body  composition  variables obtained by  
D XA  at baseline and  after 12  weeks  are presented  by  the study 
group. After 12  weeks  of intervention, considering each  group  
separately, it w as possible to observe a significant reduction in fat 
mass  (kg and  %), trunk fat mass  (kg), visceral fat and  adipose load 
index in the control group. In the probiotics group  it w as possible 
to observe significant decrease in total fat mass  (kg). In the joint 
analysis of the two  groups  throughout  the study, a significant ef- 
fect of time  for total fat mass  (kg and  %), trunk fat mass  (kg), 
visceral fat and  load capacity index w as observed. How ever, there 
w as no  significant effect of group  and  of probiotics supplementa-  
tion (effect group  vs. time)  on  the variables obtained with  D XA  
(Table 4). These  findings suggest that the reduction in body  fat 
could be attributable to caloric restriction overshadowing  any 
probiotic effect. 
Regarding the laboratory variables evaluated at the beginning 
of follow-up and  after 12  weeks  of nutritional intervention

significant effect of the group  on  TGs, which  can be attributed to

higher decrease in HbA1c  w as observed  in the probiotics group

TGs. Given  that baseline (W0)  values of TGs  wer e significantly 
different between  groups, a regression analysis w as performed. 
The  results indicated that the modifications in TGs  values during 
the study period wer e not significantly different between  pro- 
biotics and  control groups, even after adjustment  for baseline 
values of TGs.

4. Discussion
In the present study, after 12  weeks  of supplementation  with  
multiple probiotic strains, a significant change  w as observed  in 
several body  adiposity parameters  in the intragroup  analysis in 
both  groups. How ever, there w as no  significant difference  between  
the groups, in contrast to other studies in which  probiotics sup- 
plementation  produced  a significant reduction in body  adiposity 
[22 ,24–27,45,46]. Most of the studies that observed  a significant 
decrease in body  adiposity, supplemented  probiotics without  
concomitant  energy  restriction [24–27,45,46]. Whereas  among  the 
studies with  concomitant  energy  restriction the most  consistent 
finding is similar to ours, that is, absence  of significant reduction in 
adiposity [47–52 ]. In a critical review  recently published by  our 
study group, we  highlight that the significant reduction of body  
adiposity in studies with  probiotics supplementation  is observed  
more  frequently in studies without  the concomitant  energy  re- 
striction [53 ]. 
Other  factors may  have also contributed for the non-significant 
decrease in body  adiposity with  probiotics supplementation  
observed  in the present study. W e can suggest the inclusion of 
individuals presenting hypertension in use of antihypertensive 
drugs, as previous studies show ed  that the use of anti- 
hypertensive drugs may  impact  the composition  of gut micro- 
biota [29,30]. To our know ledge only one  previous study included 
specifically subjects presenting hypertension and  excessive body  
adiposity [54]. In this single study, a standard hypocaloric diet 
(1512  kcal/d) w as supplemente d with  50  g/day  of a probiotic 
product  (semi-hard cheese)  containing L. plantarum  TENSIA  for 3  
weeks  and  a significant decrease in BMI w as observed  in the 
probiotic cheese group  versus the control cheese group  [55 ]. One
the other hand, in the study conducted  by  Rom ~ ao da  Silva et al.,
2020  [54] wer e included only wom en  with  hypertension (body 
adiposity w as not an  inclusion criteria) and  after 8 weeks  of pro- 
biotics supplementation, without  energy  restriction, anthropo- 
metric variables we re similar between  the two groups. Therefore, 
at this moment  it is not clear if the use of antihypertensive drugs 
affect the potential beneficial effect of probiotics on  body  
adiposity. 
It is notew orthy that although  there are man y published 
studies evaluating the effect of probiotics on  body  adiposity, so far, 
few  of these used  gold standard methods  to assess body  compo-  
sition, such  as, D XA,  computed  tomogr aphy (CT) and  magnetic  
resonance  imaging  (MRI) [22 ,24,46,56 ,57]. Although  weight  
tracking and  BMI are commonly  used, these measur es do  not 
provide insight into the relative contributions of fat mass  and  fat- 
free mass  or the changes in these compartments.  Accur ate as- 
sessments  of adiposity can be obtained using gold-standard 
methods  by  minimizing  the influence of factors such  as hydra- 
tion. These  methods,  unlike BMI, provide detailed insights into 
body  composition, which  reduces the risk of misinterpretations 
caused  by  external variables [58]. 
A  recent publication assessed the percentage of RCT evaluating 
the effects of probiotics supplementation  reporting a significant 
decrease in body  adiposity based  on  the specific adiposity 
paramet er used. The  findings revealed that the percentage w as 
lower in studies evaluating body  weight  (32  %)  and  BMI (34 %)  
compared  to those assessing w aist circumference  (48 %), total fat 
mass  (53  %), and  abdominal  fat area (60 %)  [53 ]. Therefore, the 
beneficial effect of probiotics ma y vary according to the paramet er 
of body  adiposity evaluated.

between  gut microbiota and  glucose metabolism  include: 1) the

In contrast to previous RCTs [21,61,79], we  found  no  significant 
impro vement  in lipid profile with  probiotics supplementation  in 
comparison  with  the control group, although  there w as a signifi- 
cant decrease in serum  levels of TC in both  study groups  (intra- 
group  analysis) and  in TGs  in probiotics groups. These  results are in 
agreement  with  other trials [80–82]. It is worth  mentioning  that in 
the present study participants had  baseline mean  values of lipid 
profile close to the normal  range, which  ma y justify, at least in 
part, the absence  of beneficial effects of probiotics, since evidence 
suggests a more  pronounced  hypocholesterolemic effect in in- 
dividuals with  elevated TC and  LDL-C serum  levels [83,84]. In the 
systematic  review  and  meta-analysis of RCT conducted  by  Pour- 
rajab et al. (2020) [85], probiotic yogurt significantly reduced  TC 
and  LDL-C in subjects with  mild  to moderat e hypercholesterole- 
mia  without  a significant effect on  HDL-C and  TGs  levels. 
The  mechanisms  underlying cholesterol lowering effects of 
probiotics are not yet fully understood. Some  probiotics contain 
enzymes  capable of catalyzing the conversion of cholesterol into 
intermediates, such  as coprosterol or coprostanol, directly 
excreted  in feces [86,87]. In addition, some  probiotics may  pro- 
mot e deconjugation of bile salts in the small intestine through  the 
activity of bile salts hydrolases, leading to an  increase in the 
required amount  of endogenous  cholesterol for the synthesis of 
bile salts [88,89]. It is also discussed the ability of probiotics to 
produce  large amounts  of propionic acid, which  can lead to inhi- 
bition of hepatic cholesterol synthesis and/or  redistribution of 
circulating cholesterol to the liver [90]. 
The  strengths of this study include the study design (placebo- 
controlled and  double-blind RCT), the supplementation  with  
multiple strains of probiotics, the evaluation of body  adiposity by  
gold standard method.  Anothe r very important  strength is the 
evaluation of probiotics effects on  body  adiposity in individuals at 
risk of CVD.  In this population, weight  reduction is crucial, and  the 
use of antihypertensive drugs, which  can influence gut microbiota, 
has been  assessed in only one  prior study. Among  the limitations, 
the main  one  is the relatively small size of sample, due  to the 
COVID-19 pandemic  and  the rigorous eligibility criteria. Never- 
theless, in the present study, the effect size w as estimated using 
eta squared. None  of the statistical analyses with  non-significant 
p-values (≥0.05) show ed  a large effect size. Most of these ana- 
lyses had  small effect size (ƞ 2  <  0.06). As  effect size indices are not 
influenced by  sample  size, it can provide insight into the mean-  
ingful effects of the intervention. The y describe the size of 
observed  effects independently  of the influences of sample  size 
[91,92].
5. Conclusions
In the present study, the supplementation  of multiple strains of 
probiotics associated  with  energy  restriction in individuals with  
excess body  weight  and  hypertension, over a 12-we ek period, 
promo ted a significant impro vement  in glucose homeostasis  
assessed by  HbA1c.  How ever, no  beneficial effects wer e observed  
on  body  adiposity and  lipid profile. 
Further well-designed, large-scale, multicenter, blinded RCTs 
are needed  to enhance  the quality of evidence concerning  the
effect of probiotics on  body  adiposity in individuals with  elevated 
cardiovascular risk.
