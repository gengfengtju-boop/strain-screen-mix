Health Organization (WHO), an estimated 2.5 billion adults

as an important risk factor for chronic conditions such as

and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). T2DM is 
a chronic disease characterized by peripheral resistance to

Abstract
Introduction Synbiotic supplements have been shown to affect type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and non-alcoholic fatty 
liver disease (NAFLD), however; results remain inconclusive. Thus, the present study was designed to investigate the poten-
tial effect of multi-strain synbiotic supplements on liver enzymes, insulin resistance, anthropometric indices, and inflamma-
tory markers in overweight/obese patients with NAFLD and T2DM.
Method In a 12-week triple-blinded randomized controlled trial, 40 eligible overweight or obese adults with NAFLD and 
T2DM were randomly assigned to two groups to consume either synbiotic supplements or a placebo along a low-calorie 
diet. Participants were assessed for liver enzymes, anthropometric and glycemic indices, and lipid profiles before and after 
the study.
Result After the study period, using intention-to-treat approach 20 individuals were included in the final analysis for each 
group. The intervention group showed significant reductions in within group analysis for insulin levels, weight, and BMI 
(P < 0.05). AST was reduced in both intervention and control groups. However, no significant differences were found for 
between-group analyses. Additionally, changes in inflammatory markers, lipid profiles, and insulin resistance indices were 
not statistically significant.
Conclusion In the present study, synbiotic supplements showed improvements in insulin levels, weight, BMI, and AST. 
However, in comparison to the control group no beneficial effects were observed. Further studies are recommended to draw 
more definitive conclusions.

Recent studies show the close connection between gut 
microbiota composition and metabolic health. Dysbiosis 
of gut microbial communities has been linked to obesity-
related disorders, such as the pathogenesis of NAFLD and 
insulin resistance. Probiotics, a group of live microorgan -
isms which confer beneficial health effects after adequate 
consumption for the host, could improve gut microbiota. On 
the other hand, prebiotics are considered as substrates that 
foster the growth of beneficial bacteria. The term synbiotic 
is used to define the combined administration of probiot -
ics and prebiotics for their potential role in modulating gut 
microbiota and exerting beneficial effects on host metabo -
lism [4].
Among different bacterial genera, the Lactobacillus and 
Bifidobacterium families are used as probiotics due to their 
health-promoting effects. It has been suggested that Lacto -
bacillus acidophilus might help decrease low-density lipo -
proteins (LDL) and total cholesterol while aiding in weight 
loss [ 5]. A positive association was observed between 
Lactococcus lactis and increased antioxidant capacity and 
reduced inflammation (CRP) [ 6]. Lactobacillus rhamnosus 
has been recognized as an effective probiotic for reducing 
fasting blood glucose and HbA1C [ 7]. In addition, Bifido -
bacterium longum was shown to reduce triglyceride levels 
and improve insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) [ 8]. Bifido -
bacterium breve has been shown to promote weight main -
tenance and decrease ALT levels [ 9]. Lactobacillus casei 
was shown to decrease pro-inflammatory markers and, con-
sequently, inflammation [ 10]. In this study, a multi-strain 
synbiotic supplement containing Lactobacillus acidophilus, 
L. lactis, L. rhamnosus, Bifidobacterium longum, B. breve, 
and L. casei, combined with fructooligosaccharides as a 
prebiotic, was selected to target multiple aspects of meta -
bolic health. This combination was chosen to potentially 
influence lipid metabolism, glycemic control, liver function, 
insulin resistance, and inflammation, addressing the inter -
connected metabolic disturbances of NAFLD and T2DM in 
overweight and obese individuals.
Even though the results are promising, limited evidence 
exists about the effect of multi-strain synbiotic supplemen -
tation on liver enzymes, insulin resistance, and anthropo -
metric and inflammatory indices in overweight or obese 
individuals who concurrently suffer from NAFLD and 
T2DM. Therefore, it is crucial to fill this gap to clarify the 
clinical efficacy of synbiotics as adjunctive therapy in meta-
bolic disorders. Thus, this randomized controlled trial was 
conducted to establish the effects of supplementation with a 
multi-strain synbiotic on liver enzymes, insulin resistance, 
and anthropometric and inflammatory indices in overweight 
and obese adults diagnosed with co-occurring NAFLD 
and T2DM. To our best knowledge, this is the first triple-
blinded randomized controlled trial to evaluate the effects 
of a multi-strain synbiotic supplement in this dual-diagnosis 
population, combined with a standard low-calorie diet and 
comprehensive evaluation of metabolic and inflammatory 
outcomes, thereby opening new avenues for its potential 
role as an integrated therapeutic strategy. We anticipated 
that multi-strain synbiotic supplementation, in conjunction 
with a low-calorie diet, would significantly improve liver 
enzymes, insulin resistance, anthropometric indices, and 
inflammatory markers compared to a placebo group over 12 
weeks.
Method
Study design and participants
A 12-week triple-blind, parallel, randomized, controlled 
clinical trial using a simple randomization technique was 
designed. The study protocol followed the Declaration of 
Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board 
and Ethics Committee (approval code: IR.SUMS.SCHEA -
NUT.REC.1402.154) of the Shiraz University of Medical 
Sciences, Shiraz, Iran. The study protocol was registered 
in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials ( IRCT.ir, ID: 
IRCT20240212060977N1 on February 12, 2024).
Sample size for this current study was calculated using 
aspartate aminotransferase (AST) as the primary outcome, 
based on a study conducted by Javadi et al. [ 11]. Mean 
change from baseline in AST was − 14.47 mg/dL (post-
intervention SD = 13.65) in the Probiotic + Prebiotic arm 
and − 1.94 mg/dL (post-intervention SD 12.46) in the Pla -
cebo arm, with an effect size of 12.53 mg/dL. Using a two-
sample t-test with 80% power (β = 0.20), a two-tailed type 
1 error of 0.05 (α = 0.05), and a standard deviation of 13.65 
(as reported for the Probiotic + Prebiotic group), a mini -
mum of 19 participants per group was required, totaling 38 
participants. To ensure robustness, we rounded to 20 per 
group, yielding 40 participants. The dropout rate we experi-
enced was 7.5% (3 of the 40 participants), below that of the 
comparator study at 10.7%, and intention-to-treat analysis 
included all randomized participants.
The participants’ recruitment was done with convenience 
sampling from outpatients NAFLD and T2DM patients 
referred to the Motahari Clinic, affiliated with the Shiraz

Overweight or obese (body mass index (BMI) above 
25 kg/m²) adults aged over 18 years who were diagnosed 
with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (Grade 2 or 3 resulting 
based on the liver ultrasound results) and suffered from pre-
diabetes or T2DM with fasting blood glucose above 100 mg/
dL (who did not use insulin), were not being pregnant or in 
lactating, were not affected by any metabolic, respiratory, or 
kidney disorders, and had not used any antibiotics or probi-
otic/synbiotic supplements in the past 3 months before the 
study, were eligible to participate.
Participants were excluded if they developed respiratory 
or renal diseases during the study, consumed multivitamins 
during the study, consumed less than 80% of the prescribed 
supplements, used any probiotics or synbiotics supplements 
beside the study interventions, were required to use antibiot-
ics, became pregnant, or started insulin therapy.
Outcome
AST was considered the primary outcome of the present 
study. The secondary outcomes were other liver enzymes 
including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and alkaline phos-
phatase, lipid profile indices including low-density (LDL) 
and high-density (HDL) lipoproteins, triglycerides (TG), 
and total cholesterol, insulin resistance indices including 
fasting blood sugar (FBS), Homeostatic Model Assessment 
for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), Homeostatic Model 
Assessment of Beta Cell Function (HOMA-B), Quantita -
tive Insulin Sensitivity Check Index (QUICKI), and fast -
ing blood insulin level, and anthropometric measurements 
including weight, BMI, waist circumference (WC), hip cir -
cumference (HC), body fat, and lean body mass. Moreover, 
inflammatory status of individuals was assessed as second -
ary outcome using C-reactive protein (CRP), malondialde -
hyde (MDA), and total antioxidant capacity (TAC).
Study procedure and interventions
Using the block randomization method (1:1 blocks), eli -
gible participants, after signing the informed consent form, 
were randomly assigned to either the intervention or control 
group. The random sequence was provided and concealed 
in opaque envelopes by an independent person not involved 
in the study. Allocated groups were labeled as ‘A’ or ‘B’ 
by the same person. Participants of intervention group took 
synbiotic supplement in form of a capsule each day after 
lunch with the bacteria count of 109 CFU containing L. aci-
dophilus, L. lactis, L. rhamnosus, Bifidobacterium longum, 
Bifidobacterium breve, and L. casei, and fructooligosaccha-
rides as prebiotic (Zist Takhmir, Tehran, Iran) for 12 weeks. 
Net weight of capsule was 350 mg and FOS included was 
21 mg. Placebo group received capsules only containing 
maltodextrin (Zist Takhmir, Tehran, Iran). Both capsules 
were similar regarding shape, color, and size to blind par -
ticipants and investigators. Capsules were also marked as 
“A” or “B” in line with study groups by the same person 
who provided allocation sequence. In addition, both groups 
received low calorie diet (− 500 kcal/day) regarding their 
total calorie expenditure, calculated by using Harris-Bene -
dict formula. Dietary intake was monitored using a 3-day 
dietary recall (two weekdays and one weekend day) con -
ducted at baseline and at the end of the 12-week interven -
tion to estimate calorie intake and assess adherence to the 
low-calorie diet. Adherence was quantified by achieving the 
desired − 500 kcal/day deficit on ≥ 80% of the days based on 
recall data. 15 of the 20 (75%) in the intervention arm and 
14 of the 20 (70%) in the placebo arm reached > 80% adher-
ence, with the remaining 5 (25%) and 6 (30%), respectively, 
falling short of the target deficit calorie.
Compliance with synbiotic supplementation was deter -
mined by pill counts conducted during follow-up visits 
at weeks 4, 8, and 12, with the remaining capsules being 
returned by the participants to confirm usage. One par -
ticipant who consumed less than 80% of the capsules was 
excluded from the study and was among the three who were 
not given final analysis. All 37 subjects examined took more 
than 80% of the prescribed doses of capsules and achieved 
100% compliance in subjects under investigation.
Demographic and anthropometric and physical

lean body mass and fat mass. A non-elastic tape measure,

Results
The study was carried out from April to October, 2024. After 
assessment of 86 individuals for eligibility, 46 were not eli-
gible. Thus, 40 eligible individuals were informed about 
the study and randomly assigned into two groups ( n = 20) 
to receive either the intervention or the placebo. The study 
procedure is shown in Fig. 1. Table 1 shows demographic 
characteristics of participants.
There were significant increases in AST levels within 
both the placebo ( P = 0.014) and intervention ( P = 0.006) 
groups. No significant results were observed in between- 
and within-group comparisons for ALT and alkaline phos -
phatase. Similar results were observed for CRP, MDA, and 
TAC (Table 2).
Table 3 indicates the results for glycemic indices of 
participants. A notable finding was the statistically sig -
nificant between-group difference in insulin level changes 
during the study period ( P = 0.040), with the Intervention 
group increasing from 9.68 to 18.06 mIU/L (P = 0.044) and 
the Placebo group decreasing from 15.85 to 13.50 mIU/L 
(P = 0.723). The change in HOMA-B was near significance 
level (P = 0.051). However, no significant results were seen 
for HOMA-IR and QUICKI after the study period.
As shown in Table 4, there was a significant reduction in 
weight (P = 0.007) and BMI ( P = 0.007) after the interven -
tion in the synbiotic group, however, between-group com -
parison did not show significant results. Results for other 
anthropometric indices were not significant.
Table 5 shows the observations of the lipid profile assess-
ments. LDL level was significantly increased in the pla -
cebo group ( P = 0.032). The reduction was not significant 
in the intervention group for LDL. However, between-
group comparison of changes in LDL was not significant. 
Within-group comparisons for HDL did not show signifi -
cant changes but comparison of mean differences during 
the study period indicates a statistical difference between 
groups (P = 0.011). No significant results were seen for TG 
and cholesterol level.

Biochemical parameters
A 5-cc venous blood sample was collected from each par -
ticipant following an overnight fasting for at least 8 h before 
and after the study period. Blood samples from all patients 
were tested for FBS, LDL, HDL, TG, total cholesterol, 
alkaline phosphatase, and HbA1C using the colorimetric 
Enzymatic Kinetic Method. ALT, AST, CRP, fasting insulin, 
MDA, and TAC were assessed using an ELISA (Enzyme-
Linked Immunosorbent Assay) kit. Biochemical analyses 
were performed in the clinical laboratory of the Motahari 
Clinic, affiliated to the Shiraz University of Medical Sci -
ences. Insulin resistance was calculated using the HOMA-

Statistical analysis
All the statistical analyses were done using SPSS 22.0 using 
intention-to-treat approach. The Shapiro-Wilk test was per-
formed to assess normal distribution of data. Non-paramet -
ric statistical analyses were performed for skewed data. The 
Wilcoxon test was used for the within-group comparison. 
The Mann–Whitney U test was applied for between-group 
comparisons. The chi-square test was used for categorical 
variables. Statistical significance was defined at P < 0.05. 
Finally, the data reported as median (25th, 75th).

Conclusion
This randomized controlled trial evaluated the effects of 
12-week multi-strain synbiotic supplementation on health 
parameters in overweight or obese adults with type 2 dia -
betes mellitus and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. The 
results demonstrated that synbiotic treatment significantly 
improved weight and BMI within the intervention group, 
though no significant between-group effects were observed 
for these or other outcomes, including liver enzymes, 
inflammatory markers, lipid profiles, or glycemic indices. 
While within-group differences in glycemic indices were 
noted, their clinical relevance is uncertain. These findings 
suggest that synbiotic supplementation may have poten -
tially modest differential effect between groups in this 
patient population. Future research could be improved by 
employing larger sample sizes to increase statistical power, 
optimizing synbiotic preparations (e.g., strain mixtures or 
dosages), extending intervention duration, and employing 
other biomarkers to further define therapeutic potential and 
clinical significance in this patient population.
